Propositional logic

A proposition is a declarative statement that has exactly one truth value: true or false. Questions and commands are not propositions.

For propositions p and q, the conjunction p AND q is true exactly when both propositions are true. The disjunction p OR q is inclusive: it is true when at least one proposition is true, including when both are true.

The implication p implies q is false exactly when p is true and q is false. In every other case it is true. The implication is logically equivalent to NOT p OR q.

The contrapositive of p implies q is NOT q implies NOT p. An implication and its contrapositive are logically equivalent. The converse, q implies p, is not generally equivalent to the original implication.

De Morgan's laws state that NOT (p AND q) is equivalent to (NOT p) OR (NOT q), while NOT (p OR q) is equivalent to (NOT p) AND (NOT q).

Quantifiers

The statement "for every x in D, P(x)" is true when P(x) holds for every element of the domain D. The statement "there exists x in D such that P(x)" is true when at least one element of D satisfies P(x).

Negating a universal statement produces an existential statement with a negated predicate: NOT (for every x, P(x)) is equivalent to there exists x such that NOT P(x). Negating an existential statement produces a universal statement with a negated predicate.

The order of quantifiers can change the meaning of a statement. Over the integers, "for every x there exists y such that y > x" is true, because y can be chosen as x + 1. However, "there exists y such that for every x, y > x" is false, because there is no integer larger than every integer.

Sets and functions

The union A union B contains elements that belong to A, to B, or to both. The intersection A intersection B contains exactly the elements that belong to both sets. The difference A minus B contains elements in A that are not in B. This operation is not generally symmetric.

For finite sets, the inclusion-exclusion formula is:
|A union B| = |A| + |B| - |A intersection B|.
The intersection is subtracted because its elements were counted twice.

A function f from A to B assigns exactly one element of B to every element of A. Different inputs are allowed to have the same output.

A function is injective when distinct inputs have distinct outputs. A function is surjective when every element of its codomain is the output of at least one input. A function is bijective when it is both injective and surjective.

For finite sets, a bijection between A and B implies that |A| = |B|. Equal cardinalities alone do not imply that a particular function from A to B is bijective.
